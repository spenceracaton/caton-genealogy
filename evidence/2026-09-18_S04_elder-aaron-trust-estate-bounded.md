S04 — elder Broad Run Aaron trust/estate chain, bounded pass

Result

This stream is partial. It does not prove or exclude the elder Broad Run Aaron as the target Aaron’s father, and it does not establish that the elder Aaron died childless.

The useful bounded result is narrower:

1. the current latest certain public act for the Broad Run elder Aaron remains the August 1813 Prince William jury/tax-era evidence already in the repository;
2. a recorded deed executed 8 April 1834 calls an Aaron Caton deceased while reciting an earlier deed in which he served as trustee;
3. because the original trust deed was not recovered, its date cannot yet move the “last alive” endpoint forward;
4. the often-repeated 28 February 1825 death date remains unverified after a bounded provenance search;
5. no wife, child, heir, administrator, guardian, or successor trustee was exposed in the sources actually readable in this pass.

The resulting death interval is therefore still broad enough to permit biological paternity of a child born 16 May 1820.

1. 1834 Rappahannock release: what is actually established

The existing repo evidence note for Rappahannock Deed Book A, p. 305, image 192 records the instrument as beginning:

> This Indenture made this 8th day of April 1834 between John White of the County of Fauquier and State of Virginia of the one part, and John Thorn of the County of Rappahannock and State aforesaid, witnesseth; …

It also records the critical recital:

> in which Aaron Caton Decd was Trustee

The same note gives a Fauquier acknowledgment dated 8 April 1834 and Rappahannock recording dated 22 April 1834.

Limitation in this pass

The handoff specifically required a fresh reading of images 191, 192, and 193 and a full transcription. This session could not load the FamilySearch ARK image viewer or Full-Text Search. The GitHub connector could read the prior markdown note but could not render the retained JPG binary. I therefore did not pretend to re-read the manuscript, did not expand the prior transcription, and did not claim a recited date that is not actually visible in the existing note.

Why the trustee is mentioned

The prior note describes the 1834 instrument as a release concerning land occupied by John Thorn, with an earlier security/deed in which Aaron Caton was trustee. Structurally, that is most consistent with a reconveyance or title-clearing release after the earlier secured matter had been resolved. That interpretation is grade C until the full release and original trust are read. Nothing currently read shows a court order appointing a replacement trustee.

2. Original trust deed: not recovered

The handoff’s jurisdictional logic remains sound: Rappahannock County was created in 1833, so land in Rappahannock in 1834 could have been recorded in Culpeper when an earlier trust was executed. The Library of Virginia inventory shows a nearly annual sequence of Culpeper deed books across the relevant period, including GG (1813–1817), HH (1815–1817), II (1817–1818), KK (1818–1819), LL (1819–1820), MM through ZZ, and Deed Book No. 1 (1833–1834), plus long-run grantor/grantee indexes.

Those holdings define where the trust can be found, but they are not evidence that Aaron is or is not indexed there. FamilySearch Full-Text Search and the deed images were inaccessible in this session, and no public web result exposed the actual earlier instrument.

The next search should therefore be targeted rather than broad:

• Aaron Caton / Caton in Culpeper deed Full-Text Search and index;
• John White and John Thorn as reciprocal party checks;
• then the same party set in Fauquier deed indexes/books if Culpeper fails.

If the original instrument gives Aaron’s residence, that is also the cleanest way to test whether the 1834 trustee is the Broad Run man or another same-name person.

4. Fauquier estate route: the reported 1818–1829 gap should not be assumed

The Library of Virginia’s Fauquier inventory materially improves the search plan.

For the ordinary county will series it lists:

• Will Book 6, 1813–1817;
• Book 7, 1817–1820;
• Book 8, 1820–1824;
• Book 9, 1824–1826;
• Book 10, 1826–1829;
• Book 11, 1829–1831;
• Book 12, 1831–1832;
• Book 13, 1832–1835.

More importantly, Reel 44 is described as (Superior Court of Law) Deeds, Wills, Etc., 1809–1829, with a separate segment of Superior Court Records, 1818–1829 and a wills/inventories/accounts-of-sales segment. LVA expressly warns that this material is not indexed in the general deed or will indexes.

Therefore the reported “1818–1829 gap” should not be inherited as fact. What remains unverified is the exact image continuity of FamilySearch film 31609 / DGS 004087718; the LVA description itself says the record group contains 1818–1829 Superior Court material.

This is a high-value unresolved route because an administration, estate sale, dower proceeding, or trust-successor order could name a widow or heirs even if the ordinary will index is silent.

5. Culpeper estate/court coverage and real gaps

The LVA Culpeper inventory shows both substantial surviving coverage and genuine losses.

Relevant surviving probate routes include:

• General Index to Wills, 1749–1930;
• county Will Book F, 1809–1813;
• H, 1817–1822;
• I, 1822–1825;
• K, 1825–1827;
• N, 1833–1836;
• Circuit Court Will Book No. 1, 1811–1827;
• Circuit Court Will Book No. 2, 1831–1870.

The inventory also says a large number of loose records and some pre-1840 minute books are missing because of Civil War-era courthouse losses. Minute Books No. 7 and No. 9 are specifically marked missing, leaving real gaps around 1811–1814 and 1816–1818 in that county-minute sequence.

Accordingly, even a future negative must state exactly which surviving index/book series was read. An index absence cannot be silently upgraded to “no estate.”

6. 28 February 1825 provenance test

Public-web searches were run for the exact date in multiple forms with Aaron Caton and surname variants, plus Virginia/Prince William/Fauquier/Culpeper context. No primary record, identified abstract, cemetery transcription, newspaper notice, or other traceable source for 28 Feb 1825 surfaced.

That result is intentionally narrow. It does not show the date is false; authenticated FamilySearch image/tree routes were unavailable, and user-contributed tree data are not proof in any event. For this project the date should remain unverified until a source can be named and inspected.

7. Wife/heirs and bridge trace

No wife, child, heir, administrator, guardian, or successor trustee was named in any newly readable record in this pass. The “trace one named bridge” step therefore did not trigger.

This means the stream does not support either of these stronger claims:

• “the elder Aaron died childless”; or
• “the elder Aaron was the target Aaron’s father.”

The first is currently a narrative built from absences and household structure; the second remains a live control hypothesis because the death interval still spans 1820.

Bounded stopping point

S04 should remain partial, not closed. The next decisive acquisition is singular: the original deed of trust recited on 8 April 1834. Its date and Aaron’s residence can materially tighten both chronology and identity. If that deed dates after about August 1819, the elder Aaron remains chronologically capable of biological paternity; if an independent primary death record precedes that point, he is excluded as biological father. After the trust, Fauquier Reel 44 and the Culpeper general/circuit will series are the bounded estate routes.

No parent-child relationship is proved by this pass.
